Run 2026-08-14 for ticket 03, against production Firestore `rnui-pixellog-d1008/emails`.
Reproduce with `pnpm scrub:emails` — `scripts/scrub-email-list.ts` is the authority, this file is
its result written down.

## The arithmetic

```

dropped: bot         18
```

**29 addresses survive**, pending the verifier run described at the bottom. Ticket 09 needs this
number and ticket 10 sends to this list.

Nothing was dropped for syntax and nothing for MX: all 48 are well-formed, and all seven distinct
domains publish live MX. Across the 48 unique addresses that is 41 gmail.com, 2 yahoo.com, 1
proton.me, and 4 on their owners' own domains — matching the 41 this ticket opened with. Those
four domains are not named here: with the `dev@` note below, naming them would reconstruct a real
address, and this file is public.

## Where the addresses are

`pnpm scrub:emails` writes two files, and the split is the whole privacy design:

| File                      | Contents                                          | Committed?          |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| `scrub-result.local.json` | the 29 addresses, their `createdAt`, their docIds | **no — gitignored** |
| `scrub-survivors.json`    | the same 29 as docId + `createdAt`, no addresses  | yes                 |

This repo is public and `.scratch/` is tracked, so committing the addresses would publish personal
data permanently to GitHub — in a ticket whose whole purpose is protecting these people. A
Firestore document id identifies nobody outside the project, so the committed manifest makes the
consent dates auditable in-repo without exposing anyone; rejoin `docId` against Firestore
`emails/` when an address is actually needed. Regenerate both with `pnpm scrub:emails`.

Firestore itself was **not written to**. Map decision 10 keeps all 51 rows — cured and scrubbed,
not deleted — and every `createdAt` is load-bearing consent evidence. The scrub is a derived view,
so it can be re-run and corrected without ever having destroyed the record.

## The bot: 18 of 51 rows

The signup form and the contact form were both public, uncaptcha'd write paths, and the same
script hit both. `userFeedback` holds 23 rows; **18 are machine-generated** (random mixed-case
runs for first name, last name and message) and **every one of those 18 addresses also appears in
`emails`, within 43 seconds of its signup**. Median gap: 20 seconds. Roughly half filed the
contact form _before_ the signup, which is why the pairing is tested on absolute distance.

The five genuine `userFeedback` rows — the maintainer twice, plus three real people with real
messages — appear nowhere in `emails`. No human was caught by this rule.

Two independent methods agree. Besides the timestamp pairing, 17 of the 18 match a generator
signature: strip the dots gmail ignores, and the local part is three or more consonant-vowel
syllables plus one to three digits. Run that signature across all 48 and it finds **no address
that the pairing did not already catch** — the two methods converge, with the pairing strictly
dominant.

That claim is load-bearing for the count, so it lives in the code rather than in a command someone
once ran: `looksGenerated()` in `scripts/scrub-email-list.ts`, printed as the `signature check`
lines above on every run, and covered by tests. It is deliberately **not** a drop rule. Its useful
number is the second one — a survivor wearing the generator's shape that no submission pairs with
would be a bot the pairing cannot see, and there are none. The strictness is the point: relax the
single-consonant syllable to admit digraphs and the signature starts matching real people's names,
which is how a second opinion becomes a false one.

The one address they disagree on is the reason the ticket specified the cross-check rather than a
pattern match: it reads as an ordinary human first-name-plus-surname and no signature would ever
have flagged it. Its junk contact submission 31 seconds later is the only thing that gives it away.

Half the bot's addresses use gmail's dot-obfuscation (`a.b.c.d.12@gmail.com`), which is why
`normalise()` strips dots on gmail before comparing anything.

## The duplicates

Three rows collapsed, each keeping the **earliest** `createdAt` as its consent date:

- one address signed up three times (2025-09-22, 2025-10-22, 2026-06-18) → kept 2025-09-22
- one signed up twice, 97 minutes apart on 2025-11-28 → kept the 06:14 row

One person appears twice under **different domains** (a gmail address and a proton.me one). Those
are two distinct mailboxes with two distinct consent records and are correctly kept as two — the
Digest will reach the same human twice. Worth a manual merge before the first send if the
maintainer recognises them; not something the scrub can decide.

## The one judgement call

`dev@` on a personal developer domain is the sole role-address hit, and it is genuinely
borderline: the standard role list every bulk verifier ships flags `dev@`, but on a one-person
domain it is plausibly just that person's mailbox rather than a shared one. Dropped, because the
ticket says drop role addresses and because a role address is both harder to attribute consent to
and over-represented among spamtraps.

**This one is reversible and cheap to reverse** — delete `"dev"` from `ROLE_LOCALS` in
`scripts/scrub-email-list.ts`, re-run, and the count goes to 30. If the maintainer knows the
person behind it, that is the better answer.

## Two addresses no rule caught, flagged for the verifier

Neither is dropped and neither should be dropped on the evidence available:

- one gmail address reads as the same nonsense-syllable shape as the bot's, but has no contact
  submission anywhere near it and does not match the strict signature (it contains a consonant
  digraph the generator never produced)
- one gmail address is a short random-consonant run that fits no pattern in either direction

Both survive into the 29. The bulk verifier is what should settle them.

## Not done: the bulk verifier

Ticket 03's fifth acceptance bullet — _"a bulk verifier run over the survivors"_ — **has not run**,
and an agent cannot run it. Every option (ZeroBounce, Kickbox, NeverBounce, Emailable) needs an
account and a payment method that belong to the maintainer.

It should not be substituted with a hand-rolled SMTP `RCPT TO` probe. Gmail accepts every
recipient at SMTP time and bounces later, so the probe would return nothing useful for 44 of the
48, and probing from an unaccredited IP is itself a way to get blocklisted — the exact outcome the
bullet exists to prevent.

So: **feed the 29 in `scrub-result.local.json` to a verifier, drop whatever it marks undeliverable
or a spamtrap, and correct the surviving count here and in the ticket before ticket 10 sends.**
Until then treat 29 as provisional. `scrub-result.local.json` carries `"verified": false` to make
that explicit to anything downstream.
